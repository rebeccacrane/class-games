Case 1 · Who owns a tattoo in a video game?
NBA 2K copies players down to their tattoos
Who got paid
Take-Two, the game's publisher, pays the NBA and players a huge fee to use their names, faces and likenesses.
Who didn't
The artists who designed the tattoos. In 2016 a company holding their copyrights sued over 5 tattoos on LeBron James, Kenyon Martin and Eric Bledsoe.
Make a guess · 2020
You’re the judge. Did the game break copyright law?
A. Yes, pay the artists
B. No, the game is fine
What happened · March 2020
B. The judge ruled for the game, for 3 reasons
Too small
In play the tattoos are tiny and blurry, about 4–11% of real size. Nobody buys the game for them.
Implied OK
The artists knew players would appear on TV and in ads. The tattoos became part of how they look.
Fair use
New purpose: the tattoos were self-expression; the game copied them for realism.
Make a guess · Same company, new game
Wrestler Randy Orton’s tattoo artist sues over WWE 2K. A jury decides. What happens?
A. Game wins again
B. Artist wins big
C. Something else
What happened · 2022–2024
C. She won the argument and got nothing
2022 · Jury
$3,750
The jury found the game did infringe her copyright.
2024 · Judge
$0
Infringement stands, but no money: she couldn’t show she lost business.
Make a guess · Now flip it around
Tattoo artist Kat Von D copies a famous photo of Miles Davis onto a friend’s arm, for free. The photographer sues.
Is the tattoo copyright infringement?
What happened · 2024–now
Nobody knows yet. The courts are still deciding.
Jan 2024
A jury says the tattoo isn’t similar enough to the photo to infringe.
Jan 2026
An appeals court agrees, but two judges question the test used.
June 2026
The full Ninth Circuit throws that out and agrees to rehear the case. Pending.
Case 1 · Discuss
Where’s the line?
  1. Is a tattoo part of your body, like your nose, or someone else’s artwork?
  2. Should you need the artist’s permission to appear in a game, a movie or a post?
  3. Should a free, one-person copy be treated differently from a game that sells millions?
Case 2 · Your DNA as data
Spit in a tube, and your DNA becomes a database row
Benefits
  • Learn your ancestry, find relatives
  • Data for medical research
  • Police solve cold cases
Risks (warned in 2018)
  • Hacks, leaks and ransom
  • Insurers or lenders judging you
  • You can’t change your DNA like a password
Make a guess · 23andMe, 2023
Hackers log into about 14,000 accounts using passwords people reused from other sites.
How many people’s data did they get?
What happened · October 2023
6.9 million
people, nearly half of all 23andMe customers
How? The opt-in DNA Relatives feature let each hacked account see profiles of its genetic matches. 14,000 accounts unlocked millions of relatives.
Make a guess · March 2025
23andMe goes bankrupt. Its stuff gets sold to pay its debts. What happens to everyone’s DNA?
A. Deleted
B. Sold to a drug company
C. Sold to someone else
What happened · June–July 2025
C. Sold for $305 million, almost to B
First bid
Drug company Regeneron wins the first auction.
Reopened
A nonprofit run by 23andMe’s co-founder outbids it: $305 million.
Approved
States object that customers never consented. The judge approves it anyway.
Case 2 · Discuss
Whose data is it?
  1. Who’s to blame for the breach: users who reused passwords, the company, or both?
  2. Your DNA reveals your relatives too. Should they get a say?
  3. If a company goes out of business, should it be allowed to sell your genetic data?
Case 3 · Digital actors
Movies started bringing actors back from the dead
Furious 7 (2015)
Paul Walker died mid-filming. His brothers stood in and effects artists rebuilt his face to finish his scenes.
Rogue One (2016)
Peter Cushing died in 1994. He was digitally recreated for a major role, with his estate’s approval.
The central question: a dead actor can’t say yes. Who gets to decide?
Make a guess · Since 2019
AI now lets anyone fake a realistic face or voice. Did lawmakers do anything about it?
A. Nothing yet
B. One federal law
C. State-by-state laws
What happened · 2023–2026
C. A patchwork of state laws, including ours
2023
Actors strike for 4 months. New contract: no digital replica without consent.
2025 · California
Recreating a dead performer with AI needs the estate’s OK.
2026 · New York
Ads must disclose AI “synthetic performers.”
June 2026 · Washington
A fake of anyone’s face or voice, used without consent, violates their rights.
Make a guess · September 2025
A studio unveils “Tilly Norwood,” an actress made entirely by AI, and says talent agents want to sign her.
How did real actors react?
What happened · September 2025
The actors’ union condemned it right away
Not an actor
SAG-AFTRA said she is a computer-generated character with no life experience or emotion.
Built on others’ work
She was trained on countless real performances, without permission or pay.
Creator’s view
Her creator called her a work of art, not a replacement for humans.
Case 3 · Discuss
Who owns your face and voice?
  1. Is an estate’s permission enough to recreate an actor who never agreed while alive?
  2. Should an AI performer trained on thousands of actors get jobs? Who should be paid?
  3. Why might a fake video or voice of a regular person be harmful? Give an example.
Across all three cases
The technology moved first. The law is still catching up.
